The method

An explanation of how a citation enters the map.

What this is

The Symbolic World is a map of C. G. Jung's symbolic thought: 632 symbols, figures and concepts, joined by 2339 relations, resting on 3060 citations to his Collected Works (CW). Every claim was proposed by one AI model (Opus 5 class) after reading the CW and judged against its source paragraph by a different model (Fable 5 class); an auditor (Gemini) from a different vendor re-reads each new batch in full — 94% of all 3060 citations have been certified this way. Every claim can be considered a transaction in a ledger: the exact paragraph, a verbatim quote, and the record of which model (or expert) checked what. This is a map of assertions, not a Jungian reading.

Reading it: the stone (lapis) is claimed to be a projection of the self, Jung says so at paragraph 264 of Aion, and clicking the claim on this site shows the verbatim citation.

One corpus

Everything comes from a single source: the Bollingen edition of the Collected Works, in English. No secondary literature, no commentary, no symbol dictionaries. That keeps the map close to Jung's own text, at the cost of breadth.

The bar along the bottom of the atlas shows the corpus: one segment per volume, sized by how much verified evidence it carries, so where mining has gone deep is visible immediately. The segments enable scope selection: click one and the atlas shows only that volume's claims.

Reading the atlas

The type of node is indicated by node colour. The palette borrows alchemy's metals: gold for concepts, silver for symbols, copper for figures, iron for operations, verdigris for substances, lead for motifs. Alchemy assigned its metals to the planets; mapping node kinds is a design choice for the atlas, and not authoritative.

  • Search finds a symbol, a relation, a family, quote text, or a citation such as CW 14 §1. Typing offers completions, and choosing a symbol or a family opens it.
  • Click a node to read its connections; click a connection to open the citation itself: relation, quote, paragraph, verdicts. Copy link on any selection gives a URL that reopens it.
  • The chips beside the search box are the active filters: volume, family, voice, layer. Remove one with its ×.
  • The bar along the bottom selects volumes; each segment is sized by the verified evidence that volume carries.
  • Every citation shows a status dot: green, human-validated; amber, machine-verified default without human validation; red, disputed, on the record. These can be viewed in the ledger.

How a claim gets in

Five stations.

  1. Propose. An Opus-family model reads a collection of paragraphs and proposes claims, each with a verbatim citation.
  2. Check. A machine confirms the quote is letter-exact in its paragraph and at most 25 words, and that the relation is well formed, i.e. structured as a relationship between two nodes. Failures are then excluded.
  3. Gate. A Fable-family model judges every claim against its full source paragraph. It is hostile — i.e. it seeks to challenge aggressively — by default: the claim survives only if, in the model's judgment, the paragraph supports it.
  4. Audit. A Gemini model re-reads what survived, before any of it touches the graph. Universal for every batch since the Aion arc (mid-2026). (Earlier material was audited by sampling, and evidenced in the ledger; 2866 of 3060 citations have been audited this way as at time of publication.) When gate and auditor disagree, the auditor wins.
  5. Merge. The claim enters with its provenance stamped: dates, verdicts, corrections.

The rule underneath all five: the model that proposes a claim is never the model that signs it off. Proposer and gate are different model families (Anthropic's Opus and Fable lines); the auditor is a different vendor altogether (Google's Gemini). This is a standing arrangement.

The checks remove real material: in the most recent window, 66 proposed citations became 61 merged ones.

Human oversight

A person rules on disagreements, decides releases, and answers for what is published.

  • Gold set. 50 claims carry a human-validated mark in the ledger: a person read the cited paragraph. The rest carry the amber dot: machine-verified without human validation.
  • Adjudication. Two blind arms sort relations into 25 families: one Opus-family model, one Gemini, each reading the definitions document and the edge, neither able to see the other's answer. These arms disagree on 405 claims today. No model is allowed to break a tie; this is performed by a human in the loop: a person rules, in sittings. The rulings land in the ledger. An assignment is recorded with the ruleset version it was agreed under, because a later version can retire the rule it rested on.
  • Refusals are kept apart. No family means our vocabulary is short. Text undecided means Jung's own sentence hedges between two readings. Pooling them would let a thin vocabulary hide inside a hedge.
  • Disputes. 18 challenges are on the record with their outcomes, in the dispute log.
  • Publication. Releases, deploys and rulings are human decisions. Nothing here publishes itself.

Confidence levels

The checks are themselves checked:

  • Every one of the 3060 quotes is mechanically verified against the corpus. The verification set seeds seven deliberately corrupted claims — so far, all seven have been caught on every run. Whether a paragraph supports its claim is a judgment call made by a model. It is not a mechanical proof.
  • The family layer is checked by seeding corruptions among real assignments. It reported catching everything planted in its early runs. A family label means: two models, judging independently, agreed on the assignment of the relation to the family. Also here, in the case of disagreement, a human decides.
  • The classification vocabulary was checked outside this repository, against 8 works of scholarship: 8 of 16 treated items agreed with our families. We have also published the disagreements (the check). It tests the classification of relations into families, not whether a claim is true.
  • The two blind classification arms agreed on 86% of 2339 relations. Agreement measures whether the definitions can be followed consistently, not whether they are right: neither arm is authoritative. Definitions were drafted by one of the model families working in this pipeline.

The paper

The method was published with release v1.3.0: 248 symbols, 669 relations, 699 citations. That exact snapshot, atlas and ledger included, is preserved unchanged at /v1.3/. The live counts at the top of every page show how far the atlas has grown since, using the same methodology.
